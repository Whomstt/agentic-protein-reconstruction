\title{Agentic De Novo Protein Reconstruction}
\author{Cathal~Crowe,~University of Galway}

\markboth{University of Galway,~MSc AI Capstone,~2026}%
{Crowe: Agentic De Novo Protein Reconstruction}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Microbial communities play a crucial role in processes from organic matter decomposition to maintaining a healthy gut.
Protein profiling (proteomics) is a necessary step to understand microbial function within an ecosystem. The reconstruction
of proteins from unordered tryptic fragments is a bottleneck which is limiting the ability to perform proteomics.
An agentic AI approach using Protein Language Models (pLMs) and Large Language Models (LLMs) was built combining several
tools which promise more effective protein reconstruction. The results show that the iterated, agentic
reasoning approach performs considerably better on average than using manually predefined system parameters, while only
minimally outperforming an iterative, random search of multiple parameters on the same iteration budget without a reasoning engine. 
This research indicates that there is a much larger potential gain to come of agentic AI in the field of proteomics, especially for environments with fewer
computational constraints and richer information signals.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
agentic AI, protein language models, proteomics, de novo protein reconstruction.

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{M}{icroorganisms} are observed in almost every corner of the world, from land to sea to air.
Capturing the functional interactions that occur within a microbial ecosystem and understanding their characteristics
has given rise to many advancements in the domains of human biology, soil and marine environments, and bioengineered systems \cite{10.1111/j.1574-6941.2011.01284.x}.
In activated sludge, metaproteomic analysis was capable of distinguishing functioning and non-functioning systems from protein expression alone, demonstrating the potential
of proteomics to diagnose performance issues in engineered systems such as wastewater treatment \cite{10.1111/j.1574-6941.2011.01284.x}.
These types of analyses are constrained by the lack of available relevant genome and metagenome sequences, and in cases where none exist, de novo peptide sequencing
is required \cite{10.1111/j.1574-6941.2011.01284.x}.

A proteome is the complete set of proteins expressed by a genome under a set of given conditions; correspondingly, proteomics is the profiling of these expressed proteins 
\cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.
Metaproteomics is the equivalent profiling performed on a community level, where from a given sample not all proteins may belong to the same organism \cite{10.1111/j.1574-6941.2011.01284.x}.
Metagenomes and genomes show what an ecosystem is capable of, whereas the proteins expressed during sampling reveal their true nature.
Metaproteomics makes it possible to link community members to the functions which they carry out \cite{10.1111/j.1574-6941.2011.01284.x}.

Proteomics involves analysing protein fragments that resulted from enzymatic digestion,
and because of this, the original parent protein must be inferred from an implicit jigsaw puzzle of fragments \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.
Enzymatic digestions are not perfect, often resulting in missed cleavages and ambiguous fragments which could belong to multiple
parent proteins \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}. This motivates the need for more capable reconstruction algorithms, particularly when
no reference database entry exists for a given protein.

Agentic AI has already shown great promise in de novo protein design problems \cite{ghafarollahi2024protagentsproteindiscoverylarge}, \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}. 
The primary objective of this project was to investigate the application of agentic AI 
in reconstructing proteins from their fragments; to observe intelligence from the LLM as it receives feedback during the
reconstruction process, and its respective decision-making process to iteratively produce a better candidate solution.

\subsection{Proteins, Peptides and Proteomics}
Proteins are made up of chains of amino acids (peptides), with the complete set of proteins
expressed by an organism being its proteome \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}. The sequence
in which amino acids occur in a protein captures enough information to determine its structure and biological function
\cite{Dinh2026}. Proteins are often seen as the "language of life", capable of 
carrying out instructions that are found within DNA, serving as enzymes for biochemical
reactions, and providing the necessary support for cellular life structures \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.
All of this complexity and diversity in functionality is captured in what could be considered as a biological alphabet consisting of 20 standard amino acids, motivating
the development of sequence models inspired by Natural Language Processing (NLP) techniques \cite{Dinh2026}.

In proteomics, the analysis of proteins is typically not performed on their full form, but rather through a bottom-up
approach involving an enzymatic digestion causing a decomposition of proteins into fragmented peptides \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.
The trypsin enzyme is often used for this process due its specificity in terms of splitting (cleaving)
proteins at terminal sites of lysine (K) and arginine (R) amino acids (residues) respectively \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.
As the proteins under analysis can only be observed through its
cleaved peptide fragments, bioinformatics software must be used to infer a mapping to the most likely
parent candidates \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.

Enzymatic digestions are not perfect and often result in 'missed cleavages'; when the protein
is not cut at the site in which it should have been. Tandem mass spectrometry (MS) is the method used to detect peptides,
and these unexpected sequences formed through digestion can make
it more difficult to infer the sequence from the mass spectra \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}. A significant portion of peptides observed in
a sample remain unidentified, in fact on average as little as 25\% of spectra obtained through
experimentation can be identified, while the rest is discarded. \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}. It is
common in proteomics for there to be an ambiguous mapping between peptides and proteins as they
are not unique, making it considerably difficult to classify which parent protein the candidate peptide
belongs to \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}. 

Fortunately, in most proteomics workflow environments multiple digestions are performed on replicas of the 
same sample; with counts of five replicas or more being common place, and in clinical trials this count is
often much higher \cite{10.3389/fgene.2015.00360}.
This further motivates a need for more advanced reconstruction
algorithms, capable of mapping fragments from noisy signals to their original form, especially when
considering under-documented or undiscovered proteins that often prevail in the natural environment, including the human host.

\subsection{Protein Language Models}
Advances in NLP through Large Language Models (LLMs) have enabled a 
better appreciation for the "language of life" \cite{9477085}
through training on large datasets of proteins. Protein Language Models (pLMs) transfer the notion of using
tokens to represent amino acids in a self-supervised process, effectively learning to predict
masked amino acids from known sequences instead of word tokens \cite{9477085}.

While the training objective remains simple in nature, when performed over millions of proteins
the model begins to internalise more than just sequence patterns, but also the evolutionary structure
\cite{lin2023esmfold}. Training an additional folding head on top of a base pLM allows a transfer
of knowledge from cheaply learned internal representations before making more expensive 3D structure predictions
to finetune the output \cite{lin2023esmfold}.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have been almost entirely replaced by the transformer architecture
in the field of protein-sequence modeling since ProtBert \cite{9477085} highlighted the benefits 
of self-attention in capturing long range dependencies of proteins on a large-scale. Additionally, masked language modeling
(MLM) has shown great success in repairing incomplete protein sequences to their original states by probabilistically generating
and selecting candidate amino acids to fill in the blanks \cite{9477085}. The structural understanding
of proteins has been improved through models like ESMFold \cite{lin2023esmfold} which allow for
prediction of 3D structures directly from single protein sequences.

Despite these major advances, pLMs remain somewhat limited in their functionality. While folding models like ESMFold are
exceptionally more efficient at structural predictions than their alignment-based counterparts; performing up to
60x faster in inference time compared with models such as AlphaFold and RosettaFold \cite{lin2023esmfold}, these models
focus on predicting structures from known sequences rather than designing or inferring novel ones. Moreover,
current pLMs are fundamentally restricted by the limited viewpoint from data on known existing proteins 
which have already been produced by nature, inhibiting the potential to uncover proteins which differ
from these captured, and potentially biased patterns.

\subsection{De Novo Peptide Identification}
Identifying peptides is a non-trivial task, with an average of 75\% of spectra obtained
through experimentation remaining unidentified \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}. Furthermore, peptides do not always fragment as expected, 
or aren't fully 'cleaved' during enzymatic digestion. This poses the greatest challenge in 
peptide identification when fragments are incomplete or ambiguous \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}.

Machine learning has been successful in recovering structural information from incomplete signals
on several accounts in proteomics. Traditional CNNs such as DeepNovo only look at local neighbours
within a spectrum, where hybrid CNN-GNN models allow for long-range interconnections \cite{MCDONNELL20232732};
highlighting that new architectures which combine existing ideas in new ways
are constantly being introduced, often resulting in improved results.

Uncertainties in protein identification do not only appear on the mass spectrum level, but also on the sequence level.
In real-world experimentation, only partial views of proteins
are visible due to noise or errors in the data. MLM helps to simulate and train
a network to complete peptides with noise through randomly masking residues (amino acids)
and inferring their correct values through the surrounding context \cite{kharkar2024llmproteins}.

Well-documented species such as \textit{E. coli} have abundant training data, as such, pLMs are expected to generally perform
better on them than proteins which were not represented during training. 
Fine-tuning transformer models like BERT and RoBERTa that were originally trained purely on natural language are capable
of adapting to the task of predicting missing amino acids, while pLMs including ProteinBERT
which have already been trained on millions of protein sequences can be fine-tuned further
to improve their performance on specific species which may have been underrepresented during initial training \cite{kharkar2024llmproteins}.

\subsection{De Novo Protein Design}
De novo protein design involves creating proteins from scratch rather than modifying existing ones
which occur naturally. While creating de novo proteins that are not targeting a specific functionality is an interesting exercise,
the design of de novo proteins that match desirable structural makeups or 
target biological properties can be considered as a much more challenging, but rewarding task \cite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.adl4000}.
Proteins that occur naturally represent only a fraction of the theoretical potential number of possible
sequences; A peptide of length $L$ offers approximately $20^L$ possible sequences, most of which 
have never been observed in nature \cite{xia2025apexgensimultaneousdesignpeptide}.

ForceGen uses an interesting approach to de novo design through mapping desired mechanical properties such as
toughness and strength rather than structural properties \cite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.adl4000}. Via a
combination of a frozen pretrained pLM (ESM-2) and a diffusion model developed to operate within the pLM's
probability latent space, this protein language diffusion model (pLDM) architecture was capable of creating
novel proteins through mapping a dataset of only 7,026 curated records \cite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.adl4000}.
As a measure of novelty, a BLAST validation was done to observe whether there was a significant similarity
to known proteins from the ones generated; they found many of the generated sequences did not match any
sequences in the database of known proteins \cite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.adl4000}.

A newer alternative to diffusion-based generative models is that of flow matching models. Flow matching
is supposed to bridge the gap between stochastic diffusion and domains which require more deterministic
outcomes \cite{xia2025apexgensimultaneousdesignpeptide}. ApexGen uses a receptor-conditioned,
manifold-aware conditional flow matching framework capable of ensuring that the 3D structures of
generated de novo sequences will properly map to target binding sites; the specific region on a protein's surface
where a suitable peptide fits precisely \cite{xia2025apexgensimultaneousdesignpeptide}.
A template modeling (TM) score of 0.99 against the baseline comparison of PepFlow with 0.33 indicates ApexGen's
ability to generate near-perfect folds and a better global topology alignment \cite{xia2025apexgensimultaneousdesignpeptide}.

\subsection{Sequence Assembly Algorithms}
The problem of reconstructing original protein sequences from their fragmented peptides requires the use of
sophisticated sequence assembly algorithms to be achieved effectively. Standard approaches to proteomic assembly
make use of software which matches against known proteins from a database. Alternative approaches
often involve overlap-layout-consensus (OLC) and string graph approaches, spectral-network-based methods
and de Brujin graphs. The alternative graph-based approaches which do not rely on an existing database of
proteins is much more suitable for the application of this research.

A string graph is a representation of all that is inferable about a sequence from a collection of fragmented
readings collected from it \cite{10.1093/bioinformatics/bti1114}. String graphs are capable of removing
redundant edges from graphs without loss of sequence information, furthermore, they can filter out "contained
reads"; a reduction of roughly 40\% of reads in assembly data and 64\% of overlaps providing a significant
reduction in the size and complexity of the problem \cite{10.1093/bioinformatics/bti1114}. This graph
approach allows for a collapse of all repetitive elements into singular edges and ensures that the final
graph is "read coherent"; or a viable biological sequence formation \cite{10.1093/bioinformatics/bti1114}.

MS/MS database tools (e.g. SEQUEST, Mascot, and modern alternatives) often face computational
limitations when handling massive datasets of proteins. They also struggle when it comes to 
the task of identifying unknown or modified proteins that lack a database reference \cite{doi:10.1073/pnas.0701130104}.
Spectral networks bypass the use of databases completely through alignment of "spectral pairs"
(overlapping pairs of peptides or modified versions of the same) to form a full "spectral network" \cite{doi:10.1073/pnas.0701130104}.
Analysing the entire "spectral network" at once was shown to increase the confidence of peptide identifications
considerably, opening the door for shotgun protein sequencing \cite{doi:10.1073/pnas.0701130104}.

\subsection{Agentic AI in Proteomics}
Agentic AI has seen great success in recent times, particularly in the field of programming with tools
such as Claude Code completely changing the way in which software is built. Traditional rule-based pipelines struggle with handling noisy, uncertain feedback signals
which require manual hard-coded behaviours. An agentic system is controlled by an LLM 'brain', which is granted access to 'tools'. Most agentic pipelines follow the ReAct pattern
where an LLM receives a prompt and 'reacts' by executing the most relevant tools respective to the query
and its instruction. Sometimes tasks are so complex that it makes sense to have multiple LLM agents acting together towards a shared
objective. 

ProtAgents proposed a multi-agent system for de novo protein design which involved multiple
agents collaborating autonomously. A "Planner" agent develops a plan to solve the task, an "Assistant"
executes APIs and custom functions to compute the relevant data required to proceed, and a "Critic" is
responsible for giving feedback about the plan and analysing results produced by tools 
\cite{ghafarollahi2024protagentsproteindiscoverylarge}. Integration of diverse tools such as
OmegaFold and Chroma, physics-based simulations, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) from
relevant scientfic literature provide direct feedback signals to the GPT-4 powered agents, 
allowing them to cooperate and design novel proteins with minimal human intervention \cite{ghafarollahi2024protagentsproteindiscoverylarge}.
In several experiments the "Critic" agent was able to correct JSON formatting errors from resulting tool
calls and plan accordingly, an example of something which would have needed to be explicitly handled in a sequential
end-to-end pipeline \cite{ghafarollahi2024protagentsproteindiscoverylarge}.

VibeGen follows in the footsteps of ProtAgents, making use of multiple agents to generate de novo proteins,
but instead makes use of pLDMs \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}.
Proteins designed by the system are created by targeted vibrational properties where the "Protein Designer"
agent generates sequence candidates given the target properties and the "Protein Predictor" evaluates these
candidates from their normal mode shapes without requiring expensive physics simulations \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}.
VibeGen's success proves that even a relatively simple two-agent system produces 
considerably better outcomes than that of a single actor. The authors even suggest that LLM-powered behaviour could
enhance predictive power and mechanistic interpretability, further incentivising more work to be done in
this field \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}
While pLMs such as ProtBERT \cite{9477085} and ESMFold \cite{lin2023esmfold} are excellent models
for understanding the sequences and structures of proteins in their base forms respectively, they rely on the assumption that their 
input involves a protein sequence which is already correctly-ordered or complete. The implications of this are that the 
reconstruction of proteins with unordered tryptic fragments is not achievable
via direct use of the base pLMs alone, however, they can be used as biological indicators
to guide the reconstruction process through probabilistic and validity techniques.

MLM has demonstrated success in predicting missing amino acids from surrounding
context using Transformer-based models \cite{kharkar2024llmproteins}. This means that we can productively make use of
MLM as a technique at the junctions between fragment pairs to find plausible pairings of peptides, 
which would considerably aid the reconstruction process.

Generative implementations like ApexGen \cite{xia2025apexgensimultaneousdesignpeptide} and
ForceGen \cite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.adl4000} are effective systems capable of designing de novo
proteins. However, their aim is to design new proteins from scratch, while this research is undertaking
reconstruction of lab-observed, unknown proteins which already exist.

The application of agentic and multi-agent pipelines has shown great success for ProtAgents \cite{ghafarollahi2024protagentsproteindiscoverylarge}
and VibeGen \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}, highlighting the power of granting agents
access to 'tools' and having multiple agents cooperating to design targeted de novo proteins. VibeGen
particularly highlights the potential of an 'LLM brain' in terms of coordinating the design of a novel
protein from scratch \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}. The success of ProtAgents implicitly 
implies the utility of agentic AI being applied to other regions of protein design and construction.

\subsection{Research Gap}
pLMs are capable of inferring missing residues through a probabilistic MLM approach but require a complete or ordered input.
Graph-based assembly methods, such as creating an overlap graph can be used to effectively reduce the search
space without a reference database, however, they lack a biological indicator for unconfirmed adjacencies.
Agentic AI has been shown to improve the decision-making process for iterative systems, but only in de novo design,
and not reconstruction.

pLMs have not previously been used to determine how digested fragments will join together. Graph methods are shown to reduce
the search space considerably, but require a pairing with a utility that understands the biological plausibility of sequences.
Agentic AI has not yet been applied to the objective of de novo protein reconstruction.

Currently, it is not possible to create a full reconstruction of a protein from its fragments unless the overlap graph confirms all
adjacent edges. This would be a highly unlikely scenario in practice, unless the number of digested replicas is substantial
relative to the number of fragments in the digested protein. This is where guidance from a pLM is necessary,
allowing scoring of unconfirmed adjacencies probabilistically to determine the most likely pairing.

The proposed combination is to combine pLMs with graph-assembly techniques and agentic AI reasoning to address
the de novo reconstruction problem, rather than designing novel proteins with targeted properties from
scratch; where most related work is currently investigating.

Research questions to be addressed are as follows: Can de novo proteins be reconstructed from their digested fragments
using agentic AI? How effective is LLM-guided reasoning for the de novo reconstruction process?

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Proposed Approach}
The proposed approach is aimed at dealing with the problem of reconstructing proteins from their
fragmented tryptic peptides without a reference database. The objective is that an LLM will lead the decision-making
on the parameters used for tools to achieve reconstruction for the next iteration candidate based on validity signals and feedback from executed tools.
This autonomy allows the agent to reconstruct higher quality candidates iteratively through improved
parameter selection and observing tool responses without requiring any predefined manual processes to determine how
the search approach should change.

Building upon the the digestion process described earlier,
the pipeline effectively filters out impossible pairings through identifying invalid tryptic cleavages, confirms adjacencies through
an overlap graph, scores likely pairings through MLM junction scoring via a pLM, and iteratively improves candidates through
biological validation from a pLM's perplexity (uncertainty) signal.

One of the aims of these experiments was to identify any gain or loss from using LLM-guided reasoning
for iterative candidate improvement versus a random search from a parameter grid. Additionally, a 
deterministic (fixed) parameter configuration was evaluated, and finally as an absolute baseline; a complete
random shuffle of the fragments which were immediately evaluated without any processing
to see how closely a shuffled sequences could be to the original parent by pure chance.

\subsection{System Architecture}
The system is configured to have a shared pipeline across all variants. Each run is carried out
identically in terms of order; trypsin filter $\rightarrow$ overlap graph $\rightarrow$ junction scorer $\rightarrow$
beam search $\rightarrow$ validity scorer. What differs between runs is that the number
of attempts the variant gets (1 or 5) is varied, and also where the tool parameters get their values from (fixed, random, LLM).

The tunable settings which the agent has control over is limited to 5 parameters; 
junction masking window (window size of the junction), search mode (beam / greedy), 
beam width (no. of paths considered), edge mode (soft / hard), and confirmed-edge bonus (0-1).

A LangGraph ReAct framework served as the foundation for the agentic procedure. The initial input received
by the system is the digested unordered peptide fragments. A deterministic iteration with fixed parameters is ran first,
after which the agent has control over tuning the five parameters aforementioned in an attempt to improve the score
returned by the validity scorer.

To preserve the components of the reconstruction, a shared memory state is accessible between tools
so that each output returned acts as a signal for the proceeding one. GPT-5-mini was the model which was
selected to serve as the LLM brain, accessed through Microsoft's Foundry platform. It is a relatively powerful, but inexpensive
model which was easily accessible through Microsoft's 'Azure For Students' program which provided \$100 worth of free
credits on the platform which were used on API calls to the LLM Fig.~\ref{fig:agentic_pipeline}.
provides an overview of the procedure which the agentic pipeline follows.

\subsection{Tools and Signals}
The trypsin filter tool is used first to discard impossible pairings given trypsin digestion has
occurred. If we have two fragments; A and B, where fragment A ends in K/R and fragment B starts with P
this means that this is an impossible pairing as trypsin would never have cleaved there; trypsin never cuts
at K/R when followed by P. The elimination of these impossible pairings reduces
the average search space considerably by $\sim$6.1\% as found in initial experimentation.
Additionally, this tool flags fragments beginning with M as starting (N-terminal) candidates as translated proteins
typically begin with methionine, and all proteins in the dataset used begin with M.

Fig.~\ref{fig:trypsin_filter} illustrates the trypsin filter tool and how impossible pairings are eliminated.

\input{figures/trypsin_filter}

The overlap graph makes use of numerous digested replicas of the same protein, which are likely to contain missed
cleavages at different sites. If two fragments are adjacent across multiple replicas due to missed cleavages
it may be marked as a confirmed adjacency. This tool is capabale of confirming fragment pairings between replicas that were found in the original protein 
if duplicate peptides do not occur within the same replica, and partially confirming the rest where ambiguity occurs.
This tool significantly reduces the search space for guesswork during reconstruction. Partial confirmed adjacencies
can occur if there are multiple of the same peptide found within the same replica. A configuration of hard and soft 
adjacency interpretations are allowed for these circumstances. Hard will mean that only solutions which make use
of the confirmed pairings will be considered, while the soft mode can take a confirmed bonus parameter which determines the strength
of the partial adjacency confirmations and whether other candidates should still be considered. As the number of replicas available
increases, the number of confirmed adjacencies also rises.

Fig.~\ref{fig:overlap_graph} illustrates how an adjacency is confirmed from agreement across replicas.

\input{figures/overlap_graph}

The junction scorer tool's objective is to score all pairwise fragment permutation junctions using
MLM. This involves creating an n*n matrix (n is the count of
tryptic peptides). The first amino acid of fragment B is masked, then the MLM attempts to unmask it
when given the sequence A + MASK [1:B].
The MLM functionality is provided by the pLM ESM-2. The
model returns a probability distribution of likely amino acids which would best fill this mask.
The score is determined by probability of selecting the correct amino acid. Multiple residue masking is possible
by modifying the 'junction masking window' parameter (i.e. mask 2-3 residues in the adjacent fragment).

Fig.~\ref{fig:junction_scoring} illustrates the process of junction scoring across pairwise permutations.

\input{figures/junction_scoring}

After obtaining the score matrix from the junction scorer, the greedy / beam search tool must
be performed on the scored junction matrix.
In greedy search the most likely next fragment is picked given its score without considering
other initially less-likely paths which may lead to more probable sequences overall. 
In beam search, the top-k most probable paths
are considered to discover potentially stronger candidates than
those which appear probable at first. Setting the k to one sets this tool to a greedy search for the system.

Fig.~\ref{fig:greedy_beam_search} illustrates the method of selecting the best pairing paths to form a complete protein.

\input{figures/greedy_beam_search}

The validity scorer efficiently uses the same pLM that was used for junction scoring (ESM-2).
It takes the candidate solution proposed by the agent and returns a score which represents the likelihood of it 
reflecting the original protein. The score is
calculated through a perplexity (uncertainty) measurement gathered from the previous junction scoring step
along with an agreement reading from the confirmed adjacencies identified from the overlap graph tool. This signal is used to select the
best candidate produced by the agent, with the best scoring candidate always being selected after all 
iterations have been exhausted. The feedback from this tool directly tells the agent if the parameter
configurations are working well or not, triggering an exploration vs. exploitation
dilemma for the agent to optimise the reconstruction given its limited iteration budget. It is worth stating that this tool is incapable of 
guaranteeing that it will correctly indicate which candidate is the closest to the original, and should only be considered
as a proxy. 

Fig.~\ref{fig:validity_scorer} illustrates how competing candidate reconstructions are scored and selected.

\subsection{Datasets}
The source of data used for the experiments carried out in this project was the UniProt Reviewed (Swiss-Prot) dataset \cite{10.1093/nar/gkae1010}.
It served as a reliable source for well-curated proteins, and was easily accessible online for free.
To condense the scope of the project into something feasible given time and budget constraints, a decision was made to focus on only two organisms, \textit{Escherichia coli} (e. coli) and \textit{Saccharomyces cerevisiae} (yeast)
as their biological sequences are considerably different in terms of length and structure, with both belonging to different kingdoms of bacteria and fungai respectively. 
This decision allowed for a better understanding of the system's potential performance across species.

Filtering the UniProt curated dataset left $\sim$23,000 e. coli proteins and
$\sim$8,000 records for yeast. After filtering, each protein was fragmented into appropriate
peptides according to trypsin digestion; an enzymatic process in which a trypsin protease
cuts the protein into small peptide fragments by cleaving after lysine (K)
or arginine (R) residues, unless they are followed by proline (P). To simulate
a more realistic environment without adding unnecessary complexity, a simplified assumption that 30\% and 45\% of the time, the protein
will not be cut at a given target site for arginine (R) and lysine (K) respectively; i.e. will have a missed cleavage.
This is consistent with the findings that digestions occur more efficiently at arginine sites than lysine ones ($\sim$1.5x ratio) during
tryptic digestion as found in \cite{siepen2007prediction}; with a 42\% and 58\% split found for R and K respectively.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
A protein is cut into n fragments when it is digested. The objective of the system
is to reconstruct them so that they are back in the correct order as they were pre-digestion.
For each of the configurations considered ('Random Order', 'Fixed Settings', 'Random Search', and 'LLM-Guided)
the same set of fragments and proteins were evaluated for fairness.

The 'Random Order' approach measured a completely randomly shuffled set of fragments as an absolute baseline (i.e. what the
pipeline receives as input),
an important consideration to understand how much of the protein is already correctly positioned on average purely through chance.

The 'Fixed Settings' approach was a deterministic run to determine the need for dynamic
parameter configurations for the problem. Iteration 1 used fixed settings on all configurations to serve
as a starting point. The default configuration of parameters was 'junction\_window' 1, 'search\_mode' greedy,
'beam\_width' 25, 'edge\_mode' hard, 'confirmed\_bonus' 0.0.

The 'Random Search' approach was a stochastic configuration which chose parameters randomly from a grid of values
for each of the 5 tool values over an iteration budget of 5. This method's purpose was to discover
the limitations of parameter selection that does not reason from feedback or learn from previous
iterations. The grid allowed for the selection of values as
follows 'junction\_window' [1,2,3,4,5], 'search\_mode' [greedy, beam],
'beam\_width' [10, 25, 50, 100, 200], 'edge\_mode' [soft, hard], 'confirmed\_bonus' [0.0, 0.5, 1.0].

The 'LLM-Guided' approach made use of an LLM after each iteration to consider which parameter values should be chosen
for the next iteration to produce a potentially better candidate. The same iteration budget of 5 was given
for a fair comparison.

The 'Best Candidate' column represents the performance ceiling of the candidates generated by the
LLM-guided approach. The reason these metrics differ from the 'LLM-Guided' column is that the validity
signal sometimes mislead the agent to select a worse candidate from those which it had reconstructed.

Four key metrics were selected to remove redundancy while capturing the most information.
Adjacent Pair Accuracy serves as the core metric, considering the fraction of consecutive pairs in the reconstruction
that are also present in the original.
Exact Match is a ratio indicator of where a complete match has taken place i.e. the reconstruction is
exactly the same as the original with all fragments ordered correctly.
The Longest Correct Run metric considers the longest correctly ordered subsequence of ordered fragments, normalised by n; the number of fragments.
Edit Similarity measures the Levenshtein (edit) distance required to reach the target (repositioning amino acids), normalised by the length of the protein in residues.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Interpretation of Results}
It is clear from the results that the biggest gains in performance came from the jump from
fixed settings with one iteration to a random search of settings on an iterative budget.
It is also evident from the current results that the gap between the LLM-guided approach and
the random search is minimal.

The reasoning for this negligible increase in performance is likely due to the fact that the LLM-guided
approach only has limited control over the reconstruction process in its current form; with only 5
levers available to tune and also a limited 5 iteration budget. This means that a random search is
likely to cover a similar search space during its iterations by chance; leading to similar results.
It is likely that this gap would considerably increase if the LLM was granted more control over a
larger number of levers, however, the experiments still indicate that even with a limited selection
an LLM reasoning engine shows promise.

The biggest bottleneck can be seen through the best candidate metrics which show the selection ceiling.
This shows what the metrics would have really been if the validity signal was always right in terms of
indicating which candidate is really the closest to the original.

There is clearly a gap in reconstruction performance between both organisms, this is no surprise however,
as seen in data that both have significant differences in terms of their fragment counts. Yeast has
approximately 1.5x the number of fragments of E. coli; mean average of 32.5 and 19.3 fragments respectively
from the sampled split. This creates an objectively harder reconstructive problem.

The LLM reasoning comes at a cost which is considerable, taking approximately 5x as long to complete a
reconstruction than that of the
iterated random search approach due to intermediary LLM API calls. Whether the cost of the agentic
implementation is worth it is a debatable point, given that the current uplift in
reconstruction quality is negligible with the LLM-guided implementation, and the
uncertainty in the contribution which came from LLM-reasoning.

\subsection{Challenges and Limitations}
The validity signal is clearly the biggest challenge of reconstruction as it forces candidate selection through
a proxy which is evidently weak. The proxy of perplexity found
with junction scoring and the confirmed adjacencies with the overlap graph provide a rough indication
of which candidate is closer to the original, and is correct most of the time, however, the ceiling
demonstrates that there are huge potential performance uplifts with a stronger signal.

The experiment runs themselves were quite limited statistically in that only 100 samples were considered for
each species and only one run was performed for each configuration with the same random seed. Additionally,
the computational and timing budgets only allowed for 5 iterations per sample, potentially hiding
the true strength of the LLM's reasoning; similarly from the restricted influence with the LLM has on proceeding runs.

The scope of the project limited the experiments to consider only two species of organism,
a single pLM (ESM-2 - 8M), and no mixtures of proteins as originally planned.

\subsection{Future Directions}

The most apparent future direction for this work would be to discover a more reliable validity signal.
Folding models were initially considered as better indicators of biological plausibility and thus, better
candidates, however, computational constraints made it impossible to proceed with such models (e.g ESMFold / AlphaFold)
as even a single candidate evaluation could take upwards of 10 minutes on the hardware available.
The ceiling gap would suggest that the higher computational demand from a much richer validity signal
would be worth the reconstructive accuracy uplifts.

A larger iteration budget, more tools, and more influential control would likely see the gap between
LLM-guided reconstruction and random search grow considerably. It should also be noted that due to
the limited selection of tools available for the agent and budget constraints, a decision was made
to remove intermediary calls to the LLM between tool calls before candidate presentation; with a wider
selection of tools, allowing more freedom over the execution order of tools could yield even greater results.

To capture more statistically significant results, a larger sampling of proteins per species, and
more species considered would be a desirable for future work. Furthermore, investigating reconstruction from mixed protein samples
would be beneficial as this is more likely to be the case in real-world mixed microbial community sampling. Considering alternative pLMs (e.g. higher parameter models) and
alternative pLM architectures (e.g. decoder-only / encoder/decoder; ESM-2 is encoder-only) would be interesting to see how capable they
are at improving the reconstruction process in terms of junction scoring and guiding the best candidate selection. Brief testing during
early experimentation has already shown promising uplifts in reconstruction accuracy with a larger ESM model (ESM-2 650M).

\section{Conclusion}

The reconstruction of tryptic fragments into their original proteins without a reference database is a challenging task.
The combination of using an overlap-graph, pLM junction scoring, and LLM-guided reasoning is a novel approach to this problem.

To answer the first research question of "Can de novo proteins be reconstructed from their digested fragments
using agentic AI?", the answer is evidently yes. The APA metric went from 0.068 from the baseline random shuffle up to
0.879 with the agentic approach. It is also clear that most of the gains came from iterative search, rather than
intelligent reasoning, along with confirmed adjacencies from the overlap graph, especially when higher replica counts were available.

The picture is not as clear for the second research question of "How effective is LLM-guided reasoning for the de novo reconstruction process?".
While the current accuracy gains found from the addition of LLM-guided reasoning are minimal as seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:search_comparison_ecoli} and Fig.~\ref{fig:search_comparison_yeast},
the reasoning examples as seen in Table~\ref{tab:llm_reasoning_examples} are more promising in showing a genuine biological reasoning capability from informative signals
rather than being acts of random intuition.

Finding an effective validity signal is surely the biggest bottleneck of the system now, with only an ~64\% correct pick rate for
the best reconstructed candidate. This impediment left huge gains on the table as shown by the 'Best Candidate' metrics in Table~\ref{tab:main_results_all}.

A stronger validity signal appears to be the best way to get large immediate accuracy gains, a folding model
would be a strong starting point for serious experimentation with a large computational budget. Larger-scale experimentation
on more samples, species, and configurations would certainly yield more statisically sound results.
