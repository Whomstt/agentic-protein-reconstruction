\begin{abstract}
Microbial communities play a crucial role in processes from organic matter decomposition to keeping our guts healthy.
Protein profiling (proteomics) is a necessary step to understand the function within an ecosystem. The reconstruction
of proteins from unordered tryptic fragments is a bottleneck which is limiting our ability to perform proteomic profiling.
An agentic AI approach using Protein Language Models (PLMs) and Large Language Models (LLMs) was built combining several
tools which allow for more effective protein reconstruction. Results from the experiments confirm that the iterated, agentic
approach performs better on average than manually predefined system parameters. This research indicates that there is
a much larger potential gain to come of agentic AI in the field of proteomics, especially for environments with fewer
computational constraints and richer informational signals.
\end{abstract}

agentic AI, protein language models, proteomics, de novo protein reconstruction.

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{M}{icroorganisms} are observed in almost every corner of the world, from land to sea to air.
Capturing the functional interactions that occur within a microbial ecosystem and understanding their characteristics
has given rise to many advancements in the domains of human biology, soil and marine environments, and bioengineered systems \cite{10.1111/j.1574-6941.2011.01284.x}.
In activated sludge, metaproteomic analysis was capable of distinguishing functioning and non-functioning systems from protein expression alone, demonstrating the potential
of protein profiling to diagnose performance issues in engineered systems such as wastewater treatment \cite{10.1111/j.1574-6941.2011.01284.x}.
These types of analyses are constrained by the lack of available relevant genome and metagenome sequences, and in cases where none exist, de novo peptide sequencing
is required \cite{10.1111/j.1574-6941.2011.01284.x}.

A proteome is the complete set of proteins expressed by a genome under a set of given conditions; correspondingly, proteomics is the profiling of these expressed proteins 
\cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.
Metaproteomics is the equivalent profiling performed on a community level, where from a given sample not all proteins may belong to the same organism \cite{10.1111/j.1574-6941.2011.01284.x}.
Metagenomes and genomes show what an ecosystem is capable of, whereas the proteins expressed during sampling reveal the story of what it is actually doing;
protein profiling makes it possible to link community members to the functions which they carry out \cite{10.1111/j.1574-6941.2011.01284.x}.

Proteomics involves analysing protein fragments that resulted from enzymatic digestion,
and because of this, the original parent protein must be inferred from this jigsaw puzzle of fragments \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.
Enzymatic digestions are not perfect, often resulting in missed cleavages and ambiguous fragments which could belong to multiple
parent proteins \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}. This motivates the need for more capable reconstruction algorithms, particularly when
no reference database entry exists for a given protein.

Agentic AI has already shown great promise in de novo protein design problems \cite{ghafarollahi2024protagentsproteindiscoverylarge}, \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}. 
The primary objective of this project was to investigate the application of agentic AI 
in reconstructing proteins from their fragments; to observe intelligence from the LLM as it receives feedback during the
reconstruction process, and its respective decision-making process to produce a better candidate solution.

\subsection{Proteins, Peptides and Proteomics}
Proteins are made up of chains of amino acids (peptides), with the complete set of proteins
expressed by an organism considered as its proteome \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}. The sequence
in which amino acids occur in a protein captures enough information to determine its structure and biological function
\cite{Dinh2026}. Proteins are often seen as the "language of life", capable of 
carrying out instructions that are found within our DNA, serving as enzymes for biochemical
reactions and providing the necessary support for cellular life structures \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.
All of this complexity and diversity in functionality is captured in what could be considered as a biological alphabet consisting of 20 standard amino acids, motivating
the development of sequence models inspired by Natural Language Processing (NLP) approaches \cite{Dinh2026}.

In proteomics, the analysis of proteins is typically not completed on their full form, but rather through a bottom-up
approach involving an enzymatic digestion causing a decomposition of proteins into fragmented peptides \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.
The trypsin enzyme is often used for this process due its specificity in terms of splitting (cleaving)
proteins at terminal sites of lysine (K) and arginine (R) amino acids (residues) respectively \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.
As the proteins under analysis can only be observed through its
cleaved peptide fragments, bioinformatics software must be used to infer a mapping to the most likely
parent candidates \cite{https://doi.org/10.1002/imt2.70031}.

Enzymatic digestions are not perfect and often result in "missed cleavages"; when the protein
is not cut at the site it should have been. Tandem mass spectrometry (MS) is the method used to detect peptides,
and these unexpected sequences formed through digestion can make
it more difficult to infer the sequence from the mass spectra \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}. A significant portion of peptides observed in
a sample remain unidentified, in fact on average as little as 25\% of spectra obtained through
experimentation can be identified, while the rest is discarded. \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}. It is
common in proteomics for there to be an ambiguous mapping between peptides and proteins as they
are not unique, making it considerably difficult to classify which parent protein the candidate peptide
belongs to \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}. This further motivates a need for more advanced reconstruction
algorithms, capable of mapping fragments from noisy signals to their original form, especially when
considering under-documented or undiscovered proteomes that are often found within mixed microbial communities.

\subsection{Protein Language Models}
Advances in NLP through Large Language Models (LLMs) have enabled a 
better appreciation of the "language of life" \cite{9477085}
through training on large datasets of proteins. Protein Language Models (pLMs) transfer the notion of using
tokens to represent amino acids in a self-supervised process, effectively learning to predict
masked amino acids from known sequences instead of words \cite{9477085}.

While the training objective remains simple in nature, when performed over millions of proteins
the model begins to internalise more than just sequence patterns, but also the evolutionary structure
\cite{lin2023esmfold}. Training an additional folding head on top of the base pLM allows us to transfer
knowledge from cheaply learned internal representations before making expensive 3D structure predictions
\cite{lin2023esmfold}.

Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) have been almost entirely replaced by the transformer architecture
in the field of protein-sequence modeling since ProtBert \cite{9477085} highlighted the benefits 
of self-attention in capturing long range dependencies of proteins on a large-scale. Additionally, masked language modeling
(MLM) has shown great success in repairing noisy protein sequences to their original states by probabilistically generating
and selecting candidate amino acids to fill in the blanks \cite{9477085}. Our structural understanding
of proteins has been improved through models like ESMFold \cite{lin2023esmfold} which allow for
prediction of 3D structures directly from single protein sequences.

Despite these major advances, pLMs remain limited in their functionality. While folding models like ESMFold are
exceptionally more efficient at structural predictions than their alignment-based counterparts; performing up
60x faster in inference time compared with the likes of AlphaFold and RosettaFold \cite{lin2023esmfold}, these models
focus on predicting structures from known sequences rather than designing or inferring novel ones. Moreover,
current pLMs are fundamentally restricted by our limited viewpoint from data on known existing proteins 
which have already been produced by nature, inhibiting us from potentially uncovering proteins which differ
from these captured patterns.

\subsection{De Novo Peptide Identification}
Identifying peptides is a non-trivial task, with an average of 75\% of spectra obtained
through experimentation remaining unidentified \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}. Peptides do not always fragment as expected, 
or aren't fully cleaved during enzymatic digestion. This poses the greatest challenge in 
peptide identification when fragments are incomplete or ambiguous \cite{MCDONNELL20232732}.

Machine learning has been successful in recovering structural information from incomplete signals
on several accounts in proteomics. Traditional CNNs such as DeepNovo only look at local neighbours
within a spectrum, where hybrid CNN-GNN models allow for long-range interconnections \cite{MCDONNELL20232732};
demonstrating that new architectures which combine existing ideas in new ways
are constantly being introduced and often result in improved results.

Uncertainties in protein identification do not only appear on the spectrum level but also often on the sequence level.
In real world experimentation, only partial views of proteins and their respective peptide chains
are visible due to noise or errors occuring. Masked language modeling helps to simulate and train
a network to complete peptides with noise through randomly masking residues or single amino acids
and inferring their proper values through the surrounding context \cite{kharkar2024llmproteins}.

Well-documented species such as E. coli have abudant training data, and as such pLMs will perform
better on these species and similar ones, as they have trained on them more extensively, with the model's parameters
tuned more effectively from this luxury. Fine-tuning transformer models like BERT and RoBERTa that were originally trained purely on natural language are capable
of adapting to the task of predicting missing amino acids, while pLMs including ProteinBERT
which have already been trained on millions of protein sequences can be fine-tuned further
to improve their performance on identifying species which only had minimal training data originally \cite{kharkar2024llmproteins}.

\subsection{De Novo Protein Design}
De novo protein design involves creating proteins from scratch rather than making changes to existing ones
that occur naturally. While creating de novo proteins that are not targeting a specific functionality is an interesting exercise,
the design of de novo proteins that match desirable structural makeups or 
useful properties can be considered as a much more challenging, but rewarding task \cite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.adl4000}.
Proteins that occur naturally represent only a fraction of the theoretical potential number of possible
sequences; A peptide of length $L$ offers approximately $20^L$ possible sequences, most of which 
have never been observed in nature \cite{xia2025apexgensimultaneousdesignpeptide}.

ForceGen takes an interesting approach to design through mapping desired mechanical properties such as
toughness and strength rather than structural properties \cite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.adl4000}. Through a
combination of a frozen pretrained pLM (ESM-2) and a diffusion model developed to operate within the pLM's
probability latent space, this protein language diffusion model (pLDM) architecture was capable of creating
novel proteins through mapping a dataset of only 7,026 curated proteins \cite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.adl4000}.
As a measure of novelty, a BLAST validation was done to observe whether there was a significant similarity
to known proteins from the ones generated; they found many of the generated sequences did not match any
sequences in the database of known proteins \cite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.adl4000}.

A newer alternative to diffusion-based generative models is that of flow matching models. Flow matching
is supposed to bridge the gap between stochastic diffusion and domains which require more deterministic
outcomes \cite{xia2025apexgensimultaneousdesignpeptide}. ApexGen uses a receptor-conditioned,
manifold-aware conditional flow matching framework capable of ensuring that the 3D structures of
generated de novo sequences will properly map to target binding sites; the specific region on a protein's surface
where a suitable peptide fits precisely \cite{xia2025apexgensimultaneousdesignpeptide}.
A template modeling (TM) score of 0.99 against the baseline comparison of PepFlow with 0.33 indicates ApexGen's
ability to generate near-perfect folds and a better global topology alignment \cite{xia2025apexgensimultaneousdesignpeptide}.

\subsection{Sequence Assembly Algorithms}
The problem of reconstructing original protein sequences from their fragmented peptides requires the use of
sophisticated sequence assembly algorithms to be done effectively. Standard approaches to proteomic assembly
make use of software which will match against known proteins from a database. Alternative approaches
often involve overlap-layout-consensus (OLC) and string graph approaches, spectral-network-based methods
and de Brujin graphs. The alternative graph-based approaches which do not rely on existing knowledge of
proteins existing is much more suitable for our problem.

A string graph is a representation of all that is inferable about a sequence from a collection of fragmented
readings collected from it \cite{10.1093/bioinformatics/bti1114}. String graphs are capable of removing
redundant edges from graphs without loss of sequence information, furthermore, they can filter out "contained
reads"; a reduction of roughly 40\% of reads in assembly data and 64\% of overlaps providing a significant
reduction in the size and complexity of the problem \cite{10.1093/bioinformatics/bti1114}. This graph
approach allows for a collapse of all reptitive elements into singular edges and ensures that the final
graph is "read coherent"; or a viable biological sequence formation \cite{10.1093/bioinformatics/bti1114}.

MS/MS database tools (e.g. SEQUEST, Mascot, and modern alternatives) often face computational
limitations when handling massive datasets of proteins. They also struggle when it comes to 
the task of identifying unknown or modified proteins that lack a DB reference \cite{doi:10.1073/pnas.0701130104}.
Spectral networks bypass the use of databases completely through alignment of "spectral pairs"
(overlapping pairs of peptides or modified versions of the same) to form a full "spectral network" \cite{doi:10.1073/pnas.0701130104}.
Analysing the entire "spectral network" at once was shown to increase the confidence of peptide identifications
considerably, opening the door for shotgun protein sequencing \cite{doi:10.1073/pnas.0701130104}.

\subsection{Agentic AI in Proteomics}
Agentic AI has seen great success in recent times, particularly in the field of programming with tools
such as Claude Code completely changing the way that we build software. Traditional rule-based pipelines struggle with handling noisy, uncertain feedback signals
which require manual hard-coded behaviours. An agentic system is controlled by an LLM "brain", which is granted access to "tools". Most agentic pipelines follow the ReAct pattern
where an LLM receives a prompt and "reacts" by executing the most relevant tools respective to the query
and its instruction. Sometimes tasks are so complex that it makes sense to have multiple agents acting together for a shared
objective. 

ProtAgents proposed a multi-agent system for de novo protein design which involved multiple
agents collaborating autonomously. A "Planner" agent develops a plan to solve the task, an "Assistant"
executes APIs and custom functions to compute the relevant data required to proceed, and a "Critic" is
responsible for giving feedback about the plan and analysing results produced by tools 
\cite{ghafarollahi2024protagentsproteindiscoverylarge}. Integration of diverse tools such as
OmegaFold and Chroma, physics-based simulations, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) from
relevant scientfic literature provide direct feedback signals to the GPT-4 powered agents, 
allowing them to cooperate and design novel proteins with minimal human intervention \cite{ghafarollahi2024protagentsproteindiscoverylarge}.
In several experiments the "Critic" agent was able to correct JSON formatting errors from resulting tool
calls and plan accordingly, something which would have needed to be explicitly handled in a sequential
end-to-end pipeline \cite{ghafarollahi2024protagentsproteindiscoverylarge}.

VibeGen follows in the footsteps of ProtAgents, making use of multiple agents to generate de novo proteins,
but instead makes use of pLDMs \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}.
Proteins designed by the system are created by targeted vibrational properties where the "Protein Designer"
agent generates sequence candidates given the target properties and the "Protein Predictor" evaluates these
candidates from their normal mode shapes without requiring expensive physics simulations \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}.
While VibeGen is not agentic in terms of LLMs, it proves that even a relatively simple two-agent system produces 
considerably better outcomes than a single actor. The authors even suggest that LLM-powered behaviour could
enhance predictive power and mechanistic interpretability, further incentivising more work to be done in
this field \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis}
While pLMs such as ProtBERT \cite{9477085} and ESMFold \cite{lin2023esmfold} are excellent models
for understanding the sequences and structures of proteins in their base forms respectively, they rely on the assumption that the problem being 
dealt with involves a protein which is correctly ordered and / or complete. These assumptions do not directly work for our downstream
task in reconstruction with unordered tryptic fragments, however, they can be used as biological indicators
to guide the reconstruction process through probabilistic and validity approaches.

Masked language modeling has demonstrated success in predicting missing amino acids from surrounding
context using Transformer-based models \cite{kharkar2024llmproteins}, this means that it can be effectively
made use of in a junction scoring mechanism to find plausible pairings of peptides, guiding the reconstruction
process.

Generative implementations like ApexGen \cite{xia2025apexgensimultaneousdesignpeptide} and
ForceGen \cite{doi:10.1126/sciadv.adl4000} are effective systems capable of designing de novo
proteins. However, their aim is to design new proteins from scratch, while this research is undertaking
reconstruction of lab-observed, unknown proteins which already exist.

The application of agentic and multi-agent pipelines has shown great success for ProtAgents \cite{ghafarollahi2024protagentsproteindiscoverylarge}
and VibeGen \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}, highlighting the power of granting agents
access to "tools" and having multiple agents cooperating to design targeted de novo proteins. VibeGen
particularly highlights the capacity of an "LLM brain" in terms of coordinating the design of a novel
protein from scratch \cite{ni2025agenticendtoendnovoprotein}. VibeGen's success implicitly 
indicates the utility of agentic AI in other regions of protein construction.

\subsection{Research Gap}
pLMs are capable of inferring missing residues through a probabilistic MLM approach but require a complete or an ordered input.
Graph-based assembly methods such as creating an overlap graph can be used to effectively reduce the search
space without a reference database, however, they lack a biological indicator for unconfirmed adjacencies.
Agentic AI has been shown to improve the decision-making process for iterative systems, but only in de novo design,
and not reconstruction.

pLMs have not previously been used to determine how digested fragments will join together. Graph methods are shown to reduce
the search space considerably, but require a pairing with something that understands the biological plausibility of sequences.
Agentic AI has not yet been applied to the objective of de novo protein reconstruction.

It is not possible to create a full reconstruction of a protein from its fragments unless the overlap graph confirms all
adjacent edges; a highly unlikely scenario in practice unless the number of digested replicas is substantial
relative to the number of fragments in the digested protein. This is where guidance from a pLM is necessary,
allowing scoring of unconfirmed adjacencies probabilistically to determine the most likely pairing.

The proposed combination is to take pLMs along with agentic AI and graph-assembly techniques to address
the de novo reconstruction problem, rather than designing novel proteins with targeted properties from
scratch; which is where most related work is currently investigating.

Research questions to be addressed are as follows: Can we reconstruct de novo proteins from digested fragments
using agentic AI? How effective is LLM-guided reasoning for the de novo reconstruction process?

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Proposed Approach}
The proposed approach is aimed at dealing with the problem of reconstructing proteins from their
fragmented tryptic peptides without a reference database. The idea is for an LLM to guide the system to take decisions
on the parameters used for tools to guide reconstruction based on validity signals and tool feedback.
This autonomy allows the agent to reconstruct higher quality candidates iteratively through improved
parameter selection and observing tool responses without requiring any predefined manual process.

Building upon the the digestion process described earlier,
the pipeline effectively filters out impossible pairings through identifying invalid tryptic cleavages, confirms adjacencies through
an overlap graph, scores likely pairings through MLM junction scoring with a pLM, and iteratively improves candidates through
biological validation from a pLM's perplexity signal.

The aim of this setup was to identify any gain or loss which came with using LLM-guided reasoning
for iterative candidate improvement vs. a random search of a parameter grid, while also measuring how a 
single one-off setting configuration applied on all runs performs and finally as an absolute baseline; a complete
random shuffle of the fragments to see how closely a shuffled sequences is to the original on average.

\subsection{System Architecture}
The system is configured to have a shared pipeline across all variants. Each runs
identically in terms of order; trypsin filter $\rightarrow$ overlap graph $\rightarrow$ junction scorer $\rightarrow$
beam search $\rightarrow$ validity scorer. What differs between runs is that we vary the number
of attempts the variant gets (1 or 5), and also where the tool parameters get their values from (fixed, random, LLM).

The tunable settings which the agent has control over is limited to 5 parameters; 
junction masking window (window size of the junction), search mode (beam / greedy), 
beam width (width of the beam), edge mode (soft / hard), and confirmed-edge bonus (0-1).

A LangGraph ReAct framework served as the foundation for the agentic procedure. The input received
by the LLM is the initial unordered peptide fragments, with a first run to execute the deterministic parameters.
After the first run, the agent has control over tuning the five parameters beforementioned in an attempt to improve the score
returned by the validity scorer.

To preserve the components of the reconstruction, a shared memory state is accessible between tools
so that each output returned acts as a signal for the proceeding one. GPT-5-mini is the model which has
been selected as the LLM brain, accessed through Microsoft's Foundry platform; it is powerful and
easily accessible. Fig.~\ref{fig:agentic_pipeline}
provides an overview of the procedure which the agentic pipeline follows.

\subsection{Tools and Signals}
The trypsin filter tool is typically used first to discard impossible pairings given trypsin digestion has
occurred. Let's imagine that fragment A ends in K/R and fragment B starts with P, this tells us
that A$\rightarrow$B is an impossible pairing as trypsin would never have cleaved there. This reduces
our average search space considerably to $\sim$6.1\% found in initial experimentation.
Additionally, this tool flags fragments beginning with M as starting (N-terminal) candidates.
Fig.~\ref{fig:trypsin_filter} illustrates the trypsin filter tool and how impossible pairings are eliminated.

\input{figures/trypsin_filter}

The overlap graph makes use of multiple digested replicas of the same protein molecule, which are likely to have missed
cleavages at different sites. If two fragments are adjacent across many replicas, we can mark that as a confirmed
adjacency. This essentially provides us with guaranteed pairings that can be found in the original protein,
significantly reducing the search space for guesswork during reconstruction. It should be noted that these confirmed adjacencies
are not entirely the ground truth as there could be same-fragment sequences found within one protein, this is why we allow
for a configuration of hard / soft adjacency interpretations; where hard will mean that we never consider other candidates
that don't include the confirmed pairings, and soft can take a confirmed bonus parameter determines the strength
of the adjacency confirmation and whether other candidates will be considered. As the number of replicas available
increases, the number of confirmed adjacencies also rises.

The junction scorer tool's objective is to score all pairwise fragment permutation junctions using
masked language modelling (MLM). This involves creating an n*n matrix (n is the count of
tryptic peptides) The first amino acid of fragment B is masked, then the MLM attempts to unmask it
when given the sequence A + MASK [1:B].
The MLM functionality is provided by the pLM ESM-2. The
model returns a probability distribution of likely amino acids which would best fill this mask.
The score is determined by probability of selecting the correct amino acid. Multiple residue masking 
is also optional as a potential improvement (i.e. mask 2-3 residues in the adjacent fragment).
Fig.~\ref{fig:junction_scoring} illustrates the process of junction scoring across pairwise permutations.

\input{figures/junction_scoring}

After obtaining the score matrix from the junction scorer and considering impossible
candidates from the trypsin filter, there are greedy and beam search tools which
can be performed on the scored junction matrix.
In greedy search the most likely next fragment is picked given its score without considering
other potential paths which may lead to more probable sequences. In beam search, the k best paths
are considered at each step, expanding upon each to discover stronger candidates than
initially apparent. Setting the k to one effectively acts as a greedy search for the system.
Fig.~\ref{fig:greedy_beam_search} illustrates the method of selecting the best pairing paths to form a complete
protein.

\input{figures/greedy_beam_search}

The validity scorer efficiently makes use of the same pLM used for junction scoring (ESM-2).
It takes the candidate solution proposed by the agent and returns a biological plausibility score,
calculated through a perplexity (uncertainty) measurement gathered from the previous junction scoring step
along with an agreement reading from the confirmed adjacencies identified from the overlap graph tool. This signal is used to select the
best candidate by the agent, with the lowest perplexity candidate always being selected after all 
iterations have been exhausted. The feedback from this tool directly tells the agent if the parameter
configurations which it is trying is working well or not, triggering an exploration vs. exploitation
dillema for the agent to optimise the reconstruction. It is worth stating that this tool is incapable of 
guaranteeing that it will correctly indicate the closest candidate to the original, and should only be considered
as a proxy.

\subsection{Datasets}
As a source of data for experimentation and testing, UniProt Reviewed (Swiss-Prot) \cite{10.1093/nar/gkae1010}
served as an excellent resource for well-curated proteins. To condense the scope of the project
into something feasible, a decision was made to focus on only two organisms, Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Saccharomyces cerevisiae (yeast)
as they are well-studied and have a substantial amount of data publicly available on them.

Filtering the UniProt curated dataset left $\sim$23,000 e. coli molecules and
$\sim$8,000 records for yeast. After filtering, each molecule is fragmented into appropriate
peptides according to trypsin digestion; an enzymatic process in which a trypsin protease
cuts the molecule into small peptide fragments by cleaving after lysine (K)
or arginine(R) residues, unless they are followed by proline(P). To simulate
a more realistic environment without adding unnecessary complexity, an assumption that 30\% and 45\% of the time, the molecule
will not be cut at a given target site for arginine (R) and lysine (K) is made respectively; i.e. will have a missed cleavage.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
A protein gets cut into n fragments when it is digested. The objective of the system
is to reconstruct them so that they are back in the correct order as originally pre-digestion.
Every approach (Random Order, Fixed Settings, Random Search, LLM-Guided) are all handling the same set of fragments / samples
as each other.

Four key metrics were selected to remove redundancy while capturing what matters most.
Adjacent Pair Accuracy serves as the core metric, considering the fraction of consecutive pairs in the reconstruction
that are also present in the original.
Exact Match is a ratio indicator of where a complete match has taken place i.e. the reconstruction is
exactly the same as the original with all fragments ordered correctly.
The Longest Correct Run metric considers the longest correctly ordered subsequence of ordered fragments, normalised by n.
Edit Similarity measures the Levenshtein (edit) distance required to reach the target (repositioning amino acids), normalised by the length of the protein.

\subsection{Qualitative Analysis}
In general the reconstruction is more likely to be not be perfect than to be perfect,
however, we see that most reconstructions have a very strong APA score which means that
the system can still be useful. One point especially worth considering with these results is
that the system can help disambiguate fragments which are shared by multiple parent proteins.

While the results heavily indicate towards most of the real value coming from testing
more combinations i.e. increasing the search space, the LLM brain clearly has some merit to
it. It consistently outperfrmed or matched the random search metrics when given the same
iteration budget. This demonstrates that the LLM was able to intelligently interpret the feedback
signals from the tools and select more appropriate parameters than just randomly guessing to
produce better candidates. It should also be considered that more tools and further signals which
are more noisy would likely see the LLM outshine any random search with the same number of iterations.
